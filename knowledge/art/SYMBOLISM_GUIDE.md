# Symbolism Guide

## Purpose
Defines how recurring biblical symbols are selected, contextualized, depicted, and reviewed so they clarify Scripture without inventing doctrine.

## Governing rules
- Scripture and reviewed character context determine symbol meaning.
- A symbol may have different meanings in different passages; context must be recorded.
- Symbols support the narrative and never replace it.
- Avoid combining unrelated symbols merely to make a card appear more spiritual.
- Avoid visual claims about disputed interpretations without an explicit note and approval.

## Symbol specification
For every prominent symbol, record:
- symbol name;
- scriptural references;
- intended meaning in this asset;
- relationship to the subject;
- physical or compositional placement;
- meanings not intended;
- review concerns.

## Common symbol families

### Light
May communicate revelation, guidance, truth, holiness, hope, exposure, or divine initiative depending on context. Avoid treating every bright light as a direct depiction of God.

### Fire
May communicate God's presence, acceptance, purification, judgment, guidance, zeal, or destruction according to the passage. Render with environmental consequence rather than generic magical particles.

### Cloud
May communicate presence, concealment, guidance, glory, weather, or judgment. Do not automatically pair cloud and beam effects with every miracle.

### Water
May communicate creation, life, cleansing, danger, chaos, crossing, provision, baptism, or restoration. The selected meaning must come from the scene.

### Dove
May reference the Holy Spirit in specifically supported contexts, peace in broader cultural use, or an ordinary bird. Do not use it as a universal decoration for holiness.

### Olive branch or tree
May support peace, restoration, cultivation, endurance, provision, or regional setting. Noah-related usage requires the narrative context of Genesis.

### Crown and throne
May communicate kingship, authority, reward, rule, or ambition. Use historically plausible forms and distinguish earthly status from divine sovereignty.

### Shepherd staff
May communicate literal shepherding, guidance, care, leadership, protection, or calling. Do not assign it to unrelated leaders without rationale.

### Scroll or tablets
May communicate law, covenant, prophecy, teaching, record, decree, or Scripture. Form, handling, and historical context matter.

### Trumpet or horn
May communicate assembly, warning, worship, battle, proclamation, victory, or eschatological expectation. Specify which meaning is intended.

### Ark, altar, tabernacle, and temple objects
These carry sacred and covenantal significance. Depiction requires source review, architectural guidance, and restraint. They must not become generic fantasy treasure props.

### Bread, grain, vine, fruit, and harvest
May communicate provision, labour, blessing, famine, offering, covenant, teaching, judgment, discipleship, or communion depending on context.

### Chains, prison, and broken bonds
May communicate captivity, suffering, persecution, deliverance, or spiritual metaphor. Avoid using broken chains as an automatic visual cliché when the event does not support it.

### Lion, lamb, serpent, eagle, and other animals
Animal symbolism is highly contextual and sometimes multivalent. Confirm the scriptural role before use; do not assume a universally positive or negative meaning.

## Symbol density
Default maximum for a character card:
- one primary narrative symbol;
- up to two secondary supporting motifs;
- environmental echoes only when subtle.

## Integration methods
Preferred:
- physically present object;
- garment or material detail;
- architecture;
- landscape shape;
- reflected light or shadow;
- action and relationship;
- repeated compositional rhythm.

Discouraged:
- floating translucent icons;
- unexplained halos;
- symbol collages;
- glowing runes;
- decorative Hebrew or Greek text that has not been verified;
- cross imagery in Old Testament scenes unless the asset is explicitly typological and approved.

## Text and language
Do not place Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek, Latin, or English text inside artwork unless it has been verified for content, spelling, direction, historical appropriateness, and theological intent. Generated pseudo-text must be rejected.

## Review questions
1. Is the symbol supported by the selected passage or approved interpretation?
2. Could viewers reasonably infer an unintended doctrine?
3. Is the symbol integrated into the story rather than pasted on?
4. Is its historical form plausible?
5. Would removing it make the image clearer? If yes, remove it.
