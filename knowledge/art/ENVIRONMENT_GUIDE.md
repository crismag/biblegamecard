# Environment Guide

## Purpose
Defines how locations and backgrounds support biblical identity, historical plausibility, visual clarity, and narrative meaning.

## Environment principles
- The environment serves the subject and story rather than functioning as generic decoration.
- Geography, climate, architecture, vegetation, materials, and settlement scale should be plausible for the selected period and region.
- Background detail must not compete with the card's focal subject.
- Repeated settings should retain recognizable visual anchors while allowing different weather, time, season, and viewpoint.

## Research fields
Each environment specification should identify:
- biblical location or location type;
- approximate period;
- geographic region;
- climate and season when known;
- terrain and vegetation;
- architecture and materials;
- population density and social activity;
- scene-specific objects;
- disputed or uncertain assumptions;
- elements that must not appear.

## Core location families

### Wilderness and desert
Use varied stone, dry riverbeds, scrub, distant ridges, wind, dust, heat haze, cold night skies, sparse camps, and believable travel wear. Avoid treating every biblical wilderness as identical rolling sand dunes.

### Villages and agricultural land
Use compact stone or mud-brick buildings, courtyards, terraces, fields, vineyards, olive trees, wells, threshing areas, livestock, tools, and paths appropriate to the scene. Avoid modern street grids and oversized urban scale.

### Jerusalem and fortified cities
Show layered stone construction, gates, walls, stairs, markets, courts, roofs, and compressed urban space. Period matters: do not combine structures from unrelated centuries without explicit stylization.

### Palaces and courts
Express status through scale, textiles, carved surfaces, columns, guards, servants, vessels, and controlled access. Avoid default medieval-European castles, Gothic halls, and fairy-tale throne rooms.

### Tabernacle and temple contexts
Follow approved theological and historical guidance. Do not expose sacred interiors or objects merely for spectacle. Composition should communicate holiness, order, worship, service, or distance as appropriate.

### Rivers, lakes, and seas
Water conditions should support the narrative: calm reflection, difficult crossing, storm, fishing labour, shoreline activity, or ceremonial gathering. Maintain believable boat construction and scale.

### Mountains and elevated places
Use terrain, weather, distance, isolation, and exposure to communicate journey, revelation, refuge, conflict, or worship. Avoid impossible fantasy peaks unless the asset is clearly symbolic rather than historical.

### Caves, prisons, and hidden places
Prioritize material reality, constrained light, dampness, rough stone, limited movement, and emotional pressure. Avoid theatrical dungeon clichés.

## Environmental storytelling
Use a small number of story-bearing details:
- abandoned tools;
- footprints;
- broken walls;
- harvest abundance;
- distant armies;
- gathered families;
- weather change;
- smoke, lamps, or cooking fires;
- vessels, scrolls, nets, altars, tents, or carts.

Every detail should either establish place, support narrative, clarify scale, or reinforce mood.

## Depth structure
Preferred card composition uses three depth layers:
1. foreground framing or environmental interaction;
2. subject and primary action;
3. simplified contextual background.

Atmospheric perspective should reduce contrast and detail with distance.

## Weather and time
Weather and time are narrative tools, not automatic drama. Use dawn, noon, dusk, night, storm, mist, dust, rain, or clear sky only when they support the selected scene and preserve subject readability.

## Forbidden anachronisms
Unless explicitly required by a symbolic or modern adaptation, exclude:
- electric lighting;
- modern roads, vehicles, glass towers, machinery, or signage;
- modern fabrics, zippers, plastics, firearms, or printed books;
- medieval plate armour and European castles;
- fantasy architecture detached from the setting;
- archaeological monuments from the wrong period used as generic scenery.

## Environment continuity
Approved recurring locations should receive a location sheet recording skyline, major forms, materials, palette, vegetation, and key landmarks. Later scenes should remain coherent without becoming identical copies.
